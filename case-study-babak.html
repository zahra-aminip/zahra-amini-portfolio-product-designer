Babak Order Registration Redesign

From a Windows-based Manufacturing System to a Web Platform

I redesigned the order registration experience as part of the transformation of Babak Die Making’s legacy Windows-based manufacturing system into a web platform, with a focus on simplifying order entry, improving data quality, and reducing repetitive manual input.

01 — Overview

Project Overview

Babak Diemaking was using a Windows-based software system to manage its manufacturing operations, including customer management, order registration, production management, master data, and inventory. As the business grew and the need for access from multiple locations increased, the company decided to redesign the existing system and move it to a web-based platform. As the Product Designer, I was responsible for the end-to-end design process, from understanding the existing workflow and business requirements to designing, prototyping, testing, and refining the final solution.

The Challenge

The most complex part of the system was order registration. The legacy system used a single form to register orders for all types of products. Since each product required different specifications, this approach created several problems:

  • Incomplete order information
  • Important product details being hidden or overlooked
  • Higher risk of human error caused by manual data entry
  • Repetitive entry of customer information
  • Longer order processing time
  • Difficulty managing different product types within one generic form

The core challenge was not simply the visual design of the form. The problem was rooted in how information was structured and collected.

02 — Research & Findings

Understanding the Existing Workflow

I started by meeting with business stakeholders to understand the current workflow, business requirements, and expectations for the new system. These sessions helped me identify:

  • The main areas and priorities of the new platform
  • The existing order registration workflow
  • Pain points experienced by order-entry operators
  • The different requirements associated with each product type

Analyzing Information Structure

I also observed how order-entry operators worked with the existing system and reviewed the information they needed to collect for different products.This revealed an important pattern: Different products required significantly different sets of information, while the system tried to capture all of them through the same form. This became a key turning point in the project. I realized that the core problem was not the number of fields, but the underlying information architecture of the order registration process.

Key Insight

A single generic form could not effectively represent the complexity and variation of different products. When the structure of a form does not match the structure of the information users need to provide, users tend to compensate by entering details into free-text fields.This leads to:

  • Hidden information
  • Inconsistent data
  • More input errors
  • Less control over the quality of order information

The solution therefore needed to adapt the form to the product rather than forcing every product into the same structure.

03 — DESIGN

Solution

I designed a separate structure for defining each product and its required specifications. When an operator selects a product during order registration, the system dynamically displays only the fields relevant to that product.This approach allowed us to:

  • Keep each product's required information structured
  • Avoid overwhelming users with irrelevant fields
  • Improve data completeness
  • Make the order-entry process more predictable

Separating Product Definition from Order Registration

One of the key design decisions was separating Product Definition from Order Registration. Instead of embedding product specifications directly into a generic order form, product types and their required attributes were defined independently and then connected to the order registration flow. This created a more scalable structure that could support different products without making the order form increasingly complex.

Reusing Customer Information

During workflow analysis, I discovered another hidden source of inefficiency: operators repeatedly entered the same customer information for every order. This not only increased the time required to create an order, but also created opportunities for typing errors and inconsistent customer data. In the redesigned flow, the order form was connected to the customer's profile. Once the operator selected a customer, existing information such as contact details was automatically loaded into the order. This reduced repetitive data entry while improving consistency and data accuracy.

04 — Testing

Interactive Prototype & Usability Testin

After designing the new flow, I created an interactive prototype covering the complete order registration process—from selecting a customer to entering product-specific information.The prototype was not created only to demonstrate the interface. It was used to validate the underlying interaction logic before development. I focused the usability testing on questions such as:

  • Can users understand the difference between Product Definition and Order Registration?
  • Do users understand why different products display different fields?
  • Can they complete an order without unnecessary guidance?

Usability testing was conducted with the primary users of the system: order-entry staff. The results showed that users could understand the new structure and distinguish between product definition and order registration. The dynamic display of product-specific fields also helped them identify and complete the information required for each product more confidently.Testing before development helped reduce implementation risk and gave the team greater confidence in the proposed solution.

Iteration: Discovering a New Requirement

The usability testing also revealed an unexpected scenario. In some cases, the shipping information for an order was different from the information stored in the customer's profile. Users therefore needed a way to enter shipping details independently from the customer's main profile.Based on this observation, I revised the form structure and introduced a dedicated Shipping Information section.This allowed operators to specify, for each order:

  • Recipient information
  • Shipping address
  • Delivery details

without being constrained by the customer's default profile information.This was an important reminder that usability testing can reveal not only usability problems, but also missing product requirements.

05 — Result & Learning

Outcome

    The redesigned order registration flow helped users:
  • Complete product-specific information with less ambiguity
  • Follow multi-product orders more easily
  • Capture order information in a more structured way
  • Reduce repetitive customer data entry
  • Complete orders more efficiently

More importantly, the project established a more scalable information architecture for the web platform by separating product definition, customer information, and order-specific data. The usability testing also helped validate the core solution before development and uncover an additional requirement around shipping informatio

What I Learned

This project reinforced an important principle for me as a Product Designer: Not every UX problem is a UI problem. Sometimes the root cause lies deeper—in the structure of the process, the way information is collected, or the information architecture of the product.In this project, simply redesigning the visual appearance of the existing form would not have solved the underlying problem. Separating Product Definition from Order Registration and creating product-specific forms had a much greater impact on clarity and usability.I also learned the value of validating product decisions with real users before development. Testing the prototype helped us identify ambiguity early, reduce implementation risk, and discover a requirement that had not been identified during the initial research.For me, this project reinforced the value of investing in research, information architecture, and usability testing before moving into development.
